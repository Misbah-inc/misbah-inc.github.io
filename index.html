How Is This Calculated?

The Lunar Calendar and the New Crescent

The Islamic lunar calendar is based entirely on the cycles of the Moon. Each new month begins with the sighting of the young crescent moon (hilāl) shortly after the astronomical new moon — the precise moment when the Moon passes between the Earth and the Sun (conjunction). Because the Moon is between us and the Sun at conjunction, it is invisible; the crescent only becomes observable once the Moon has moved far enough east of the Sun to be seen above the western horizon after sunset.

The mean length of a lunar month (synodic period) is 29 days, 12 hours, 44 minutes, and 2.9 seconds — or about 29.53059 days. This is why Islamic months alternate between 29 and 30 days.

Step 1 — New Moon Time: The Meeus Algorithm

We compute the precise moment of astronomical conjunction (new moon) using the algorithm developed by Belgian astronomer Jean Meeus in his landmark work Astronomical Algorithms (1991). The central formula expresses the Julian Ephemeris Day (JDE) of the new moon as a function of the lunation index k (counted from the reference new moon of January 6.75, 2000 UTC, which is k = 0):

JDE₀ = 2451550.09766 + 29.530588861 × k + 0.00015437 × T² − 0.000000150 × T³

where T = k / 1236.85 is time measured in Julian centuries. To this base value, a series of trigonometric correction terms are added to account for the gravitational perturbations of the Sun (solar anomaly M), the Moon itself (lunar anomaly M'), and the orbital geometry (argument of latitude F and longitude of ascending node Ω):

JDE = JDE₀ − 0.40720 sin(M') − 0.17241 sin(M) + 0.01608 sin(2M') + 0.01039 sin(2F) − 0.00739 sin(M'−M) + 0.00514 sin(M'+M) + ...

The result is accurate to within ±2 minutes for dates within a few centuries of our epoch. The Julian Day is then converted to a standard calendar date and UTC time for display.

Step 2 — Crescent Visibility: The Yallop Criterion

The astronomical new moon itself is never visible — the Moon is too close to the Sun and lies below the horizon at sunset. The young crescent first becomes detectable in the western sky after sunset once two conditions are met: the Moon must be sufficiently old (far enough from the Sun in angular terms), and it must be above the astronomical horizon when the Sun is at a standard depression angle of 4° below the horizon (civil dusk). We apply the Yallop criterion, developed by B. D. Yallop at the UK Nautical Almanac Office (1997), which defines a single visibility parameter q:

q = [ ARCV − f(W) ] / 10

Here, ARCV (Arc of Vision) is the altitude of the Moon's centre above the astronomical horizon at the moment the Sun's centre is 4° below that horizon. W is the topocentric angular width of the lunar crescent in arcminutes — a measure of how thin (or thick) the crescent is. The polynomial function f(W) encodes the empirical relationship between crescent width and the minimum visibility altitude:

f(W) = 11.8371 − 6.3226 W + 0.7319 W² − 0.1018 W³

The computed q-value is then placed into five visibility categories:

q > 0.216A — Easily visible with the naked eye
0.216 ≥ q > −0.014B — Visible under perfect atmospheric conditions
−0.014 ≥ q > −0.160C — May need binoculars to locate; naked eye possible
−0.160 ≥ q > −0.232D — Visible with optical aid only
q ≤ −0.232E — Not visible even with optical aid

For this homepage widget, visibility is approximated from the computed lunar age at sunset in each region — a fast, reliable proxy that correlates closely with the Yallop q-value for the majority of cases. The full Yallop computation requires precise ephemeris data for the observer's latitude and longitude, which is beyond the scope of this summary display.

Step 3 — Why Different Regions See the Crescent on Different Nights

Because the Moon moves eastward relative to the Sun at approximately 12° per day, the crescent grows older — and therefore larger and higher above the horizon — as sunset advances westward across the time zones. A region that experiences sunset 3 hours later than another will observe a crescent that is 3 hours older and therefore generally more visible. This produces a predictable east-to-west gradient of visibility:

Eastern regions (East Asia, Australia) see the crescent first on a given calendar date — but because their sunset occurs earliest in UTC, the crescent may still be very young and thus difficult or impossible to see. Western regions (the Americas) experience sunset much later in UTC, giving the crescent more time to age — often making it visible in the Americas even on the same calendar date when it failed in the east.

Seasonal and latitudinal effects also matter: in spring in the Northern Hemisphere, the ecliptic makes a steep angle with the western horizon after sunset, pushing the crescent high and making it more visible. In autumn, the ecliptic is shallow, and the young Moon hugs the horizon, making sighting harder even for a mathematically favorable crescent.

Predicted Month Start

The date shown above is the first evening after the new moon on which the crescent is calculated to be potentially visible in any part of the inhabited world, based on the computed new moon time and regional sunset hours. The new Hijri month is considered to begin at the onset of that evening (the Islamic day begins at sunset).

The color coding on the map and in the regional table reflects the predicted visibility for the first eligible evening in each region: green indicates the crescent should be visible to the naked eye; gold indicates it may require optical aid; red indicates the crescent will not be visible and observers must wait for the following evening.

Religious Note: This tool provides astronomical prediction only — it is not a religious ruling (fatwa). Different marājiʿ al-taqlīd (senior Islamic jurists) may begin the month based on local sighting, a global sighting, a calculated new moon, or the methodology of a designated astronomical authority. Some scholars require direct testimony of sighting; others accept calculation. Always follow the ruling of your marjaʿ for religious observances such as the beginning of Ramadan, Eid al-Fitr, and Muharram. When in doubt, consult your local Islamic center.
